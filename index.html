A short guide

New here?

No technical background needed. In a couple of minutes you’ll know what Dynamics Lab is, why it matters and how to get started.

What is Dynamics Lab?

Dynamics Lab is an interactive learning tool for exploring how sensors respond when the quantity they measure changes, such as a temperature that rises or a pressure that drops.

Every experiment is a mathematical simulation that runs in your web browser. You don’t need any sensor hardware: you choose the settings, and the page calculates and draws how a sensor would respond.

Why does this matter?

Picture carrying a thermometer from a cool hallway into a warm kitchen. The kitchen is warm the moment you walk in, but the reading doesn’t jump there. It climbs gradually and only settles after a while.

Until it settles, the number on the display isn’t yet the true temperature. Many sensors need time to respond; the exact response depends on their dynamics. Knowing how quickly it catches up tells you when a reading can be trusted, and when it’s worth waiting a moment longer.

What do “input” and “output” mean?

Input
The real quantity being measured, such as the actual temperature of the kitchen. In the experiments you decide how it changes: all at once, at a steady pace, or up and down again and again.
Output
What the sensor reports: its output or displayed reading. In these dynamic models, the output responds over time rather than following a change instantly.

The temperature illustrations below assume a calibrated sensor whose input and displayed reading use the same units. In the general module model, compare the output with the ideal reading for the selected gain.

What does the gap mean?

In the calibrated temperature example, there is a gap between the actual temperature and the reading. During a steady climb, you can examine that gap vertically as a reading error or horizontally as a ramp time lag:

Reading error

How far the reading is from the true value at the same moment. It is measured in the units of the quantity, for example “the display shows 2 °C less than the kitchen”.

Ramp time lag

How far behind in time the reading tracks a steady climb, measured in seconds. After the initial response, compare when the input and output reach the same value. This is not a fixed waiting time before the sensor starts responding.

For the first-order example shown, once steady ramp tracking is reached, a faster climb makes the reading error larger while the ramp lag stays the same, provided the sensor settings are unchanged. Module 1 compares the output with its ideal reading and shows both quantities.
